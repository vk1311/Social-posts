**Image idea:** a plain index card with "6 questions. Same order. Every candidate." on a desk, Staffkin colours.

Unpopular opinion: your first three hires do not need an applicant tracking system.

They need the same six interview questions, asked of every candidate, in the same order.

That's it. Same questions make it easier to compare what people told you. They keep you focused on the job. And they stop the conversation from drifting toward whoever you happened to click with.

### 2. Culture fit
**Image idea:** big quote text: "Culture fit" usually means "reminds me of us." Underneath: Hire for the job.

Unpopular opinion: "we hire for culture fit" is often how small teams end up hiring people just like themselves.

It feels harmless. You want someone who gets the place. But "fit" usually means "reminds me of us."

Try this instead. Get clear on what the job needs. Write down the skills, the tasks, the first 90 days. Ask every candidate the same questions. Then hire the person who can do the work.

### 7. Two truths and a lie: hiring paperwork
**Carousel idea:** slide 1 "Two truths and a lie: hiring paperwork"; slides 2–4 one claim each; slide 5 "The lie is #3" with the explanation.

Two truths and a lie about hiring paperwork. Spot the fib.

1. New hires fill out a federal TD1 and a provincial TD1 so you can work out payroll deductions.
2. When an employee stops getting paid, you issue a Record of Employment. The deadline is short, often five calendar days.
3. A probation period means you can end employment for any reason, with no rules at all.

The lie is number 3. Probation doesn't switch off employment standards or human rights law, and the details vary by province.

Hollis can draft the paperwork. You still make the calls.

Not legal advice.

Two truths and a lie about interview questions in Canada.

1. You can ask, "Are you legally able to work in Canada?"

### 11. Reference-check tip
**Carousel idea:** slide 1 hook; slides 2–4 one question each; slide 5 "One to never ask".

Reference checks work best when you ask for specifics, not opinions. Three questions to try:

1. "What was their biggest strength on the job?"
2. "What kind of support helped them do their best work?"
3. "Would you work with them again? Why or why not?"

Then pause after each answer. A little silence gets people talking.

One to never ask: anything about health, age, family plans or other personal matters. It has nothing to do with the job, so leave it out.

Hollis can draft your reference-check questions before the call. You decide who to hire.

#HiringTips #ReferenceChecks #SmallBusinessCanada #HRForSmallTeams #Staffkin

### A. Your First Hire in Nova Scotia: A Plain-Language Checklist
1. Before you post: confirm you are ready to be an employer (budget, role, hours, pay).
2. Register with the CRA for a payroll account under your business number, to deduct and remit CPP, EI and income tax.
3. Check whether you need to register with WCB Nova Scotia.
4. Labour Standards Code basics: minimum wage, vacation pay, public holidays, pay statements.
5. Write a clear job posting that follows the Nova Scotia Human Rights Act (Hollis can draft it; you decide).
6. Put the offer in writing: pay, hours, start date, probation, duties.
7. Day-one paperwork: SIN, federal and provincial TD1 forms, and safe storage.
8. After the hire: safety orientation, record keeping, and the Record of Employment when a job ends.

Intro: Hiring your first person is a big step. It is also a bit of a paperwork pile, and nobody hands you a map. If you run a small shop, farm, studio or office in Nova Scotia, you may be wondering what you have to do before day one, and what can wait. This checklist walks through it in plain language. We start with registering as an employer, then move to pay, safety, and the documents you will need. Some steps are quick. A few take longer, so start early. Along the way, we point out where Hollis, our AI HR deskmate, can draft things like a job posting or an offer letter. You still choose who to hire and what to offer. Think of it as a short list, not a legal exam.

### B. Reference Checks for Small Employers: How to Do Them Fairly in 15 Minutes
1. Why reference checks matter, and why rushed ones go wrong.
2. Get the candidate's consent first, and agree on who you will call.
3. Know what is off limits: questions tied to protected grounds in your province.
4. Use the same five or six core questions for every finalist.
5. Run the call in 15 minutes: two-minute intro, ten minutes of questions, three-minute wrap-up.
6. Take plain, factual notes: what was said, not your impressions.
7. Store notes safely, and only as long as you need them.
8. Hollis drafts the question list and note template; you review the answers and decide.

Intro: Reference checks are the step small employers often skip when the week gets busy, or do in a rush and trust a gut feeling. That is where unfairness creeps in. You do not need an HR department to do this well. You need consent, the same core questions for every finalist, and about 15 minutes per reference, plus knowing what to leave out, like age, health, family plans or religion. This post walks through a simple, repeatable routine. Hollis does the paperwork. You do the people part.

### C. Same Questions, Fairer Hiring: Structured Interviews for Small Teams
1. Why gut feeling feels reliable, and where it trips us up.
2. What a structured interview is: same questions, same order, same notes for every candidate.
3. Why it matters more on a small team.
4. How to build one: five to seven questions tied to the real work.
5. How to take notes before you form opinions.
6. Keep questions about the job; check your province's human rights rules.
7. Where Hollis helps: it drafts the question sheet and note template. It never scores, ranks or decides.
8. Making the call: compare notes as a team, then decide.

Intro: You meet someone for thirty minutes. They are friendly, they laugh at your joke, and they remind you of your best hire. By the end, you have made up your mind. A gut feeling is easy to steer: a firm handshake or a shared hometown can matter more than what the person can do. A structured interview is a simple fix: same questions, same order, written notes, then compare. Hollis can draft the paperwork. The decision stays with you.

### D. WCB Premiums in Plain Words: What a Small Employer Actually Pays
1. What WCB is: workplace injury insurance employers fund, and the trade-off it creates.
2. What a premium is, and who pays it (the employer).
3. How it's worked out: payroll, industry class and a rate per $100 of payroll.
4. Why a roofer and an accountant pay different rates.
5. Payroll caps, and discounts or surcharges from claims history.
6. Who must register and when, including owners, contractors and family.
7. Practical steps: register on time, report payroll honestly, set aside the cost, keep records.
8. Where Hollis fits: a new-hire checklist so WCB registration isn't missed. Link the free WCB premium calculator (staffkin.com/wcb-premium-calculator/).

Intro: You hired your first person. Then a letter shows up about workers' compensation, and a word you may not know: premium. Here is the short version. WCB is insurance that employers pay into. If a worker gets hurt or sick because of the job, it helps cover their wages and care. In return, the worker generally can't sue you. The premium is usually based on your payroll and the kind of work you do. This post walks through it in plain words, so it doesn't surprise you.
